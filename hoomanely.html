// DESIGN EXERCISE · MOBILE · DOG HEALTH

The First Thirty Days

Hoomanely needs a month of a dog's data before it truly knows her. How do you design a home screen for the thirty days when the product honestly can't say much yet, without faking it and without making the owner feel they bought something that hasn't switched on?

Role
Product Designer (solo)
Time
5-hour design exercise
Platform
Mobile · 393 × 852
Scope
One home screen, three moments in time

// 01 — CONTEXT

A product that can't be rushed

Hoomanely is a health membership for dogs. A sensor-equipped bowl reads a dog's ordinary moments (meals, water and rest) and builds a Living Baseline: a sense of what's normal for that specific dog, updated as she ages.

The catch is physical, not technical. A baseline takes about thirty days of her data to form. Before that, the system knows dogs like her, not her. Every honest company in this space faces the same wait.

"We will not fake day one, and we will not let a paying customer spend a month feeling like they bought something that hasn't switched on yet." — THE BRIEF

The job: design the app's home screen and show it at three points in time. Day 1, hours after setup, when the system only knows breed, age, weight and location. Day 6, when rhythms are emerging but not settled. And Day 30, when the baseline has formed. Day 6 must not look like a broken Day 30. Each day should feel like the product working correctly on that day.

// 02 — THE CONSTRAINTS

Show time passing, without a progress bar

The brief took away every tool a designer would normally reach for to say "please wait". That's what made it interesting.

Banned patterns

No countdowns

Progress bars, % complete, ring fills, "Day 14 of 30", confetti, cartoon dogs, glassmorphism.

Claims

No promises

Never promise a date when something will be detected. Never imply the system diagnoses anything.

Voice

Calm and precise

Affection is allowed when talking about the dog, never when talking about the system, the data or the science.

Copy

Copy is half the exercise

Real sentences at real lengths. No lorem ipsum, no placeholders. Words like AI-powered, monitors, tracks, smart bowl and peace of mind were off-limits.

The dog

Meet Bella

Four years old, Border Collie mix, lives in Denver. It's late October and the weather has turned. She's left two meals half-finished. Nothing is wrong with her.

// 03 — THE RULE

One screen, one rule: who it's allowed to talk about

Instead of designing three screens, I designed one rule and let the three states fall out of it. The layout never changes. What changes is how much the system has earned the right to say.

WHAT GOVERNS HOW THE SCREEN CHANGES

The surface never gains or loses a section. All three states carry the same four: her day, her last thirty meals, the reference, and the assistant. What changes is who each sentence is about, and how strongly the system is entitled to speak.

Day 1 speaks about dogs like her. Day 6 speaks about her only where something has actually repeated: her mornings, and nothing else yet. Day 30 speaks about her, against her own range.

Every element obeys that one permission. A badge fills when its number stops belonging to her breed and starts belonging to her. Her portrait resolves as the system learns her, and coarsens again when a season or an age shifts her patterns, so it never completes. Nothing counts toward thirty.

The assistant leads while the surface cannot speak, and recedes once it can.

// 04 — THE THREE STATES

Same screen, three levels of confidence

DAY 1 · HOURS AFTER SETUP

Designing the emptiness, not apologising for it

Speaks about: dogs like her
  1. Facts, no judgement. She ate at 8:15 this morning and has drunk twice since. On day one the system can report what happened, but not whether it's normal, so that's all it says.
  2. Borrowed reference, clearly labelled. "Dogs Like Her" gives ranges for her breed, size and climate: Denver's dry air pushes that up; the cold pushes it down. The badges are outlined because these numbers belong to her breed, not to her yet.
  3. The assistant leads. While the screen has little to say, Ask EverWiz steps forward with three questions a new owner is likely to have. It fills the gap with something useful instead of a loading state.
  4. A portrait that isn't finished. Bella's photo starts as a coarse halftone, a quiet signal that the system is only beginning to see her.

DAY 6 · MID-FORMATION

Speaking only where a pattern has actually repeated

Speaks about: her, where it's earned
  1. The first real observation. That's five mornings running within twenty minutes of the same time. Mornings are the only thing that has repeated, so mornings are the only thing it talks about.
  2. Honest about what it doesn't know. Her mornings are settling first. Water and rest still move around too much to call. Saying "not yet" plainly reads as a product working correctly, not a broken one.
  3. A record, not a countdown. Each dot is a meal she ate, filled if she finished it. The row has no target and no end, so it can't be read as "12 of 30".
  4. Her own lines appear. Small traces of her meals, water and rest sit next to the reference ranges, so she starts to show up against her breed.
  5. The assistant steps back. Down to two questions, and they're about her now: Why do mealtimes settle first?

DAY 30 · BASELINE FORMED

Comparing her to herself, calmly

Speaks about: her, against her own range
  1. Noticing without alarming. She ate once today. That's two fewer than her usual three, and the first time this month. It's specific because the system now knows her "usual".
  2. Context instead of a diagnosis. One day outside her usual isn't a pattern. The product never claims to diagnose. It gives the owner a calm frame for what they're seeing.
  3. The missing meal is drawn, not hidden. On the timeline, the skipped meal appears as a dashed outline in the slot she usually eats, so you see the absence at a glance.
  4. Her normal, in plain words. She eats between 6:50 and 7:30 most mornings… She rests longest between one and four, and that has held through the cold.
  5. Judgement handed to the right person. The assistant now asks When is a change worth a vet visit?, pointing to the vet instead of pretending to be one.

// 06 — THE DIRECTION I KILLED

The ladder: a progress bar in disguise

My first idea showed the baseline as a ladder that gained a rung for every meal, drink and rest, so the record visibly stacked up over the month.

I dropped it for two reasons. It handed the owner a pile of readings and left the interpreting to them, which is exactly the work the product exists to do. And a stack that only ever grows is a progress bar with the numbers taken off, the very pattern the brief ruled out.

// 07 — WHAT I TOOK AWAY

Restraint is a design decision

01

Design a rule, not screens

One governing rule made the three states consistent and easy to defend: every element answers to the same question.

02

Copy is the interface

With no bars or rings allowed, the sentences carried the progress. Precise, calm wording did the work that visuals usually do.

03

"Not yet" builds trust

Saying plainly what the system doesn't know yet made what it does say more believable.
