// GAME UI · LIVE-OPS · MONETISATION

Battle Pass: a season worth the grind

My first step into game UI. I designed a seasonal battle pass end to end, from how players earn XP and claim rewards to how they upgrade, skip tiers and recover when they're short on gems. It's an exploration, and I learned as much from what didn't work as from what did.

BATTLE PASS HUB

// 01 — THE SYSTEM

A 90-day season, three reward tracks

Players earn XP from matches and missions and climb 100 tiers. Everyone gets the Free track, Premium adds a second track of rewards, and Premium+ extends the season with 50 extra tiers and an XP boost. The UI had to make that economy feel exciting while staying honest about what's free and what costs money.

Progress

Always know where you are

Season timer, current tier and an XP bar sit at the top of every screen, so progress is never more than a glance away.

Rewards

Four clear states

Every reward is locked, unlocked, claimed or premium-locked, and each state needs to read instantly, even at thumbnail size.

Purchases

No dead ends

Upgrading or skipping tiers always has a way forward. If you're short on gems, the next step is the shop, not an error.

// 02 — ON PAPER FIRST

Thinking it through by hand

Before any pixels, I mapped the whole system in my notebook: the XP economy, entry points, every screen and pop-up, reward states, and the open questions, like whether reward details should open in a split screen or a pop-up.

// 03 — FLOWS

Four flows, one hub

Everything starts and ends at the hub: missions, purchasing the pass, claiming rewards and skipping tiers. Each flow covers its edge cases, like an insufficient balance, and every success pop-up returns you to the hub by closing, continuing, or automatically once the animation ends.

// 04 — WIREFRAMES

Structure before spectacle

Low-fidelity layouts locked the hierarchy, and let me compare the two reward-detail options side by side before committing.

// 06 — LOOKING BACK

What I'd do differently

I wanted the season to feel loud and unapologetically girl-power, so I pushed pink everywhere. Looking back, it went too far: when the whole frame is saturated, reward rarity and state stop standing out, and the screens start to feel busy.

01

Theme in the art, not the frame

Next time I'd keep the bold theme in characters and backgrounds, and let a calmer UI frame carry states and actions.

02

Colour should mean something

In a reward system, colour is information: rarity, ownership, what's claimable. Spending it on mood costs clarity.

03

The thinking held up

The system, flows and edge cases are what I'm proudest of. Game UI is a different language, and I'd like to keep learning it.
